# Dynamic (behavioural) effects of an equal-yield shift from French property-related taxes to a land value tax

*Research note for the LVTShift pedagogical site (spec 0005, replacement scenarios). Drafted 26 October 2026. English, with proposed French site wording in section 8.*

**Purpose.** The site is a static model. It does not simulate behaviour, but it flags each expected dynamic effect with three things: a direction, a mechanism, and a range only where credible empirical work supports one. This note gathers that evidence and grades it. It does not argue for or against the reform.

**Scenarios** (cumulative, equal yield, static base):

- **S1.** The LVT replaces the taxe foncière (TFPB + TFPNB) and the DMTO.
- **S2.** S1, plus IR/IS and prélèvements sociaux on real-estate capital gains. A toggle can also replace the IFI.
- **S3.** S2, plus IR and prélèvements sociaux on rental income (revenus fonciers, and furnished lets if a yield source exists).

**Verification legend.**

- **[P]**: I read the number in the primary source (abstract, summary page or full text).
- **[S]**: the number comes from a reliable secondary source that quotes the primary; the secondary is named.
- **[NV]**: not verified. Either the source was blocked or paywalled, or I could not find the number. Numbers marked [NV] should not go on the site.

**Credibility scale.**

- **Strong**: several credible quasi-experimental studies that agree, or settled theory plus supporting evidence.
- **Moderate**: one or two credible studies, or consistent evidence from settings unlike France.
- **Weak**: correlations, cross-country regressions, single-city case studies, or model simulations.

### 1.2 The five things that matter most

1. **The DMTO is the largest efficiency lever, but its size in France is uncertain.** Abroad, transfer taxes clearly reduce transactions and homeowner mobility. Two quantitative assessments put the welfare cost at about €1 per €8 of revenue (Toronto) and 12–15 % of revenue at a 2 % rate (Helsinki model), rising steeply with the rate. The French rate on existing dwellings is about 5.8–6.3 %, so in principle the cost should be large. Yet the only French quasi-experiment, the 2014 rise from 3.8 % to 4.5 %, found a short timing effect and no lasting effect on volumes.
2. **Capitalisation is the dominant "dynamic" effect, and it is distributional.** Recurrent land taxes are largely or fully capitalised into land prices (full in Denmark). Owners at the time of announcement bear the LVT, and owners of assets whose taxes are removed gain. At equal yield the two roughly offset across all owners (§5.4); what the reform changes is who gains and who loses. The present-value arithmetic is done elsewhere; the evidence here supports modelling it at 100 %, with a sensitivity case around 60 %.
3. **Construction effects are plausible in direction but weakly evidenced, and probably muted in France.** Most LVT evidence comes from small Pennsylvania cities with weak identification. French housing supply is much less price-elastic (about 0.25–0.5) than US supply (about 1.75), largely because of land-use regulation.
4. **An LVT is not shifted to tenants; the evidence that removing taxes on landlords lowers rents is thin.** Rent effects should be shown as "≈ 0 short run, possibly slightly ↓ long run, not quantifiable".
5. **GDP-level claims should be avoided or heavily qualified.** The OECD "tax-and-growth" ranking comes from cross-country panels with weak identification and fragile robustness. It also concerns shifts from income taxes in general, mostly on labour, not a swap among property-related taxes.

## 2. Effect 1: Transactions and residential mobility

### 2.1 Tax removed: DMTO (all scenarios)

**Direction.** ↑ transaction volumes, ↑ homeowner mobility, ↑ prices (see §5). A one-off timing distortion is likely around the change date.

**Mechanism.** A transfer tax is paid each time a property changes hands. It drives a wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers receive, which deters marginal moves and re-matching of households to dwellings. Removing it lowers the cost of moving and of trading up or down.

**French context.**

- The DMTO on existing dwellings combines a départemental rate (cap raised from 4.5 % to 5 %), a communal rate of 1.2 %, and a state levy on the départemental amount. The total is about 5.8 % at the 4.5 % cap. With the départemental rate at 5 %, a notaires' chamber reports a total of 6.3185 % (Nord, from 1 May 2025) [P], which is consistent with the formula.
- Article 116 of the loi de finances pour 2025 allows départements to raise their rate to 5 % for deeds signed between 1 April 2025 and 31 March 2028, with an optional reduced rate or exemption for first-time buyers [S: LégiFiscal]. A bank information note reports 84 départements at 5 % by 1 June 2025 [S: LCL; not checked against the official list].

**Evidence.**

**Assessment.**

- That transfer taxes depress transactions and homeowner mobility is one of the best-established results in housing public finance. The international studies agree on direction and use credible designs.
- On size, the long-run semi-elasticities cluster around **−6 % (Germany) to about −14 % (Toronto: −15 % for 1.1 %) per percentage point**.
- **There are two reasons not to put a single number on the site:**
  1. **Extrapolation.** Every quasi-experiment changes the rate by 0.5–2 pp. Full removal of the French DMTO is about 5.8–6.3 pp, far outside the observed range, and effects are unlikely to be linear.
  2. **France contradicts the foreign estimates.** Bérard & Trannoy find a re-timing effect and no lasting volume effect from the 2014 rise. The French increase was small, which leaves room for low statistical power, but the result counsels caution.
- The welfare cost per euro of revenue rises with the rate in theory and in the Helsinki model. On that logic, France's high rate makes the DMTO a costly tax per euro raised. This is a reasoned transfer from foreign evidence, not a French measurement.

**Credibility.** Direction: strong. Size abroad: moderate. Size for France: weak.

### 2.2 Tax added: LVT (all scenarios)

**Direction.** ≈ directly. Possibly slightly ↑ indirectly.

**Mechanism.** A recurrent tax on land is due whether or not the property is sold, so it adds no wedge at the point of sale. Indirectly, capitalisation lowers land prices and therefore deposit needs. Best & Kleven stress that leverage amplifies transaction taxes, which suggests the reverse can ease entry. A higher holding cost on under-used land can also bring more of it onto the market.

**Evidence.** None quantifies this channel directly. **Credibility:** weak (theory only).

### 2.3 S2 addition: removal of capital-gains taxation

**Direction.** ↑ sales of long-held second homes, rental properties and land. A short-run surge is likely.

**Mechanism.** The French regime exempts the main residence. Other properties get a holding-period allowance leading to exemption after 22 years (IR) and 30 years (prélèvements sociaux). That gives owners an incentive to wait, which abolishing the tax removes.

**Evidence.**

- **Shan (2011), *J. Public Econ.*:** after the US exempted most housing gains in 1997, the semi-annual sales rate of houses with gains up to $500k rose by 0.40–0.62 pp, i.e. **+19–24 %**. The short-run effect was much larger than the long-run one. A $10,000 rise in capital-gains tax cut the sales rate by 6–13 % [P].
- **Cour des comptes (Nov. 2025), *L'imposition des plus-values immobilières des particuliers*:**
  - The retention effect is "imputée" to the tax but "peu étayée".
  - The 2011–2013 tightening, which extended full exemption from 15 to 30 years, was followed by about −19 % transactions from the 2011 peak. The Cour treats interest rates as the main driver.
  - Budget yields of possible reforms are overstated if behaviour is ignored.
  - Yield averaged about €3.5bn/yr over 2017–2023, €4.7bn in 2022 and €3.6bn in 2023 [P; Cour's summary as extracted].

**Credibility.** Moderate (US). Weak for France, where the Cour itself finds the evidence thin.

## 3. Effect 2: Construction and land use

### 3.1 Taxes removed

- **TFPB (S1).** The TFPB is assessed on the cadastral rental value of land plus buildings, so it taxes structures. Removing that part raises the return on building and improving: more units per plot, more renovation, more height ("improvement effect").
- **TFPNB (S1).** Today the TFPNB is a low tax on undeveloped land, assessed on dated cadastral values. Replacing it with an LVT at market land value would raise the holding cost of buildable land well above the current TFPNB. **The direction for buildable land depends on how the LVT values land that is zoned constructible.** That is a design choice the site should state explicitly.
- **Gains taxes (S2).** Removing them reduces the incentive to keep buildable land off the market. The Cour des comptes reports that local actors flagged this risk in the opposite direction, if the specific tax on land made constructible were sharply raised (ch. I, p. 21) [P]. There is no quantification.
- **Taxes on rents (S3).** Removing them raises landlords' after-tax yields. French evidence on rental-investment tax incentives shows such gains mostly **capitalise into prices** rather than raising construction:
  - Chapelle, Vignolles & Wolf (2018): removing the Borloo/Robien credits in part of France slowed price growth and cut vacancy in new dwellings. **New housing production did not fall** [P abstract].
  - Bono & Trannoy (2019): the Scellier relief raised building-land prices by about **8–10 %**, and by about 25 % over two years in the Greater Mediterranean region [P summary].
- **Side effect of S3.** Removing taxation of rents also removes the **déficit foncier** mechanism, under which renovation spending in excess of rents is deducted from other income. That weakens a current incentive for landlord renovation, including energy renovation. Direction ↓ for renovation incentives. Not quantifiable here.

### 3.2 Tax added: LVT

**Mechanism.** A tax on land value that does not depend on what is built cannot be avoided by building less. Taxing land instead of buildings therefore removes the tax penalty on structures. In theory a pure land tax is neutral on the timing of development if owners can borrow (Dye & England 2010, pp. 9–10). In practice, a higher holding cost on idle land is expected to speed up development.

**Evidence.**

| Study | Setting | Result | Identification | Ver. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oates & Schwab (1997), *National Tax Journal* | Pittsburgh, land/building rate ratio raised from 2:1 to 5:1 in 1979–80 | "Pittsburgh experienced a 70 percent increase in the value of building permits" in the 1980s, against declines elsewhere | Single city, concurrent development programme. The authors call it only "consistent with" a tax effect | [S] Dye & England 2010, pp. 21–22 |
| Plassmann & Tideman (2000), *J. Urban Econ.* | 15 Pennsylvania split-rate cities vs comparators, 1972–94 | **About +3–4 %** residential construction | Bayesian panel. Small cities | [S] Dye & England 2010, p. 22. Primary paywalled [NV] |
| Banzhaf & Lavery (2010), *J. Urban Econ.* | Pennsylvania tracts 1970–2000 | Split-rate raises housing-unit growth by **about 2 to 10 pp per decade** depending on the model. Mostly more units rather than bigger units | Panel with controls. Matching estimates not significant | [P] Lincoln WP 2008 version. Journal abstract has no numbers |
| Yang (2014), Lincoln WP | Pennsylvania 1970–2010, including repeals | Positive short-run effect on construction value and permits per area. Lagged effects insignificant | Panel | [P] |
| **Lyytikäinen (2009), *J. Urban Econ.*** | **Finland 2001: municipalities could add a higher rate on undeveloped residential land** | **About +12 % single-family housing starts** | Municipal panel, policy adoption | [P] abstract |
| Gemmell, Grimes & Skidmore (2019), *J. Real Estate Fin. Econ.* | Auckland 2010: rating base switched from land value to capital value | **Little evidence of effects on new building.** Some effect on alterations. Only two post-reform years | Difference-in-differences | [P] Motu summary |
| Cho, Kim, Lambert & Roberts (2013), *Am. J. Agric. Econ.* | Nashville, ex-ante simulation | Projected density +18 % to +83 % | **Simulation, not an observed effect** | [P] abstract |
| Taranu & Verbeeck (2022), *Land Use Policy* | Review of ex-post studies | Effects depend on design and local context. Dwelling-size and improvement effects can offset each other | Review | [P] abstract |

**Denmark, Estonia, New Zealand.**

- Denmark has levied a municipal land tax (grundskyld) for about a century and Estonia since 1993.
- I found **no credible causal estimate of construction effects** for either country. The Danish evidence is about capitalisation (§5).
- In New Zealand, the one quasi-experiment found little effect on new building (Gemmell et al. 2019).

**France-specific moderator.** Chapelle, Eyméoud & Wolf (2023 WP) estimate a French housing-supply elasticity of about **0.25–0.5** on average:

- about 0.45 for Paris, rising to almost 1 without land-use regulation;
- about 0.3 for Nice and about 0.8 for Nantes;
- compared with about 1.75 for the US (Saiz 2010) [P].

With supply that inelastic and planning rules (PLU) often the binding constraint, a tax change that slightly lowers the cost of building would deliver smaller volume responses in France than in US studies.

**Assessment.** The direction (more building and more intensive use) is supported by theory and by most studies. The strongest single design (Finland) concerns a tax on *undeveloped* land, which fits the TFPNB → LVT part of S1. No credible estimate of size exists for France.

**Credibility.** Direction: moderate. Size: weak.

## 4. Effect 3: Rents (landlord-tax incidence and LVT incidence)

*The incidence of housing allowances (APL; Fack 2005 vs IGEDD) is covered in a separate note and is not repeated here.*

### 4.1 Theory

- **LVT.** The supply of land is fixed, so a tax on land value cannot be passed on through lower supply. Its burden falls on landowners at the time it is announced (Dye & England 2010, p. 8), through a lower land price. Tenants' rents are set by market conditions, which the LVT does not change. Under a pure LVT, rents are not expected to rise. This is textbook and uncontroversial.
- **Taxes on structures and on landlords' income.** These are passed on only to the extent that they reduce the supply of rental housing. In the long run, supply of structures is more elastic, so part of the tax can reach tenants. Where supply is inelastic, as in most of France, landlords and owners bear more of it and it is capitalised into prices.
- **French legal point.** The TFPB cannot legally be recharged to tenants, except for the household-waste levy (TEOM). Any shifting has to happen through market rents.

### 4.2 Evidence on pass-through of property and landlord taxes to rents

| Study | Setting | Result | Ver. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Schwegman & Yinger (2020), US Census CES WP 20-43 | New York Homestead Tax Option. Within-unit panel | **About 14 %** of a tax increase passed to renters. The authors note earlier studies often found more than 60 % | [P] abstract |
| Tsoodle & Turner (2008), *Real Estate Econ.* | US, American Housing Survey hedonic model | One standard deviation higher property tax rate → about **+$400/yr rent** | [P] abstract |
| Löffler & Siegloch (2021), ZEW DP 21-026 | About 5,200 German municipal reforms | **Full pass-through to rents after three years.** Lower where supply is inelastic | [P] abstract. **Caveat:** in Germany the Grundsteuer may legally be recharged to tenants as an operating cost (Betriebskosten). Landlords captured part of the occupants' tax cut | [P] synthesis |

### 4.3 By scenario

- **S1.** Land-value incidence is not shifted, and the TFPB is not legally rechargeable. Short-run rent effect ≈ 0. In the long run, untaxing structures could add slightly to supply and put mild downward pressure on rents. In France that response is likely small (§3.2).
- **S2.** Removing the gains tax raises the expected return on owning, which shows up in asset prices. Rent effect ≈ 0.
- **S3.** Removing IR, prélèvements sociaux and CSG on rents (€7.7bn, `yields.py` after the Gemini review) raises net yields substantially. French evidence on rental tax incentives suggests the first effect is **higher prices for rental property and land**, not lower rents. Lower rents in the long run would require a supply response, for example conversion of vacant or second homes, or more investment. That is plausible but unmeasured.
- **S3 tenure point.** Today, owner-occupiers' imputed rent is untaxed while landlords' rents are taxed. S3 removes that asymmetry, which could favour rental supply relative to owner-occupation. Direction only.
- **S3 equal-yield offset.** Because the swap is equal yield, the LVT rate rises in S3. Landlords in high land-value areas may pay more LVT than they save, so the net effect on individual landlords is heterogeneous.

**Credibility.** That the LVT is not shifted to tenants: strong (theory consensus). Size of rent effects from removing landlord taxes: weak. Foreign pass-through estimates span about 14 % to 100 %, and there is no French estimate for income taxes on rents.

### 5.3 Taxes on capital income and wealth (S2, S3)

- **Rental-investment tax incentives in France.** These are capitalised into land prices (Scellier: about +8–10 %; Bono & Trannoy 2019) and into house prices (Chapelle et al. 2018) [P].
- **Wealth taxes.** In Brülhart, Gruber, Krapf & Schmidheiny (2022, *AEJ: Policy*), about **20 %** of the response of reported wealth to Swiss wealth-tax cuts comes from capitalisation into house prices [P]. This is relevant to the IFI toggle.
- **Removing the IFI.** By analogy with Sweden (Elinder & Persson 2017), the price effect may be concentrated in the top segment. This is a pattern, not an estimate.

### 5.4 By scenario

*Section reviewed and rewritten for the house view (see the review note at the end).*

The reform is equal-yield, so the total tax on property does not change; it moves. Capitalisation therefore redistributes value more than it creates or destroys it.

- **S1.** The LVT adds about €18bn a year of recurrent tax on land, and the DMTO, about €18bn a year paid at sale, disappears. If both are fully capitalised, **total property values are roughly unchanged** (the DMTO evidence above suggests its removal raises prices by about the present value of the duty, or more). That condition is demanding: the price today must reflect the whole stream of duties that every future buyer will pay, discounted at the same rate as the land tax. If buyers capitalise only the duty on their own purchase, or discount later duties more heavily, the DMTO's removal raises prices by less than the LVT lowers them, and average values fall. The swap also redistributes between properties: the DMTO weighs most, per year of ownership, on property that changes hands often, the LVT on property with valuable land, however rarely it is sold. The TFPB already taxed land and buildings; moving its building share onto land lowers the value of land-heavy properties and raises that of building-heavy ones. **Net price changes differ by land share:** properties where land is most of the value (centres, coast, Île-de-France) lose relative to those where buildings dominate.
- **Land versus buildings.** Whether the *land component* falls depends on the horizon. In the short run, existing buildings keep their own tax relief, so the land component of a given property falls. In the long run, buildings are worth what they cost to build, and the relief on buildings ends up in what a plot is worth to a builder, offsetting the fall. The site's wording on this point is pending (PR #40).
- **S2.** The extra LVT replaces taxes on gains, which owner-occupiers do not pay on their main residence. Second homes, rental property and land held for sale gain; **owner-occupied homes lose**, because they pay a higher LVT and save nothing. If the IFI is removed, high-end real estate gains.
- **S3.** Same logic for rents: landlords' net yields rise, so rental property gains; owner-occupiers, whose imputed rent is untaxed today, pay more LVT and save nothing. S2 and S3 therefore shift burden from landlords and investors to owner-occupiers. The aggregate offset holds only if the removed income taxes capitalise as fully as the LVT, which the French evidence (Scellier, Robien/Borloo) supports in direction but not in size.

**Recommendation for the model.** Use **100 % capitalisation** as the central case, supported by the land-specific Danish evidence and by full capitalisation at plausible discount rates (Palmon & Smith; Koster & Pinchbeck). Show a **60 %** sensitivity case (Oates; Palmon & Smith at 3 %; Lyu ≥71 %). Note the Swedish counter-example, where a national cut was not capitalised outside the top segment.

**Credibility.** Recurrent land tax: strong. Transfer taxes: moderate to strong. France-specific: moderate (two studies, both partial).

## 6. Effect 5: Long-run GDP level

**Framing.** Only **level** effects (a one-off rise in the long-run path) are discussed, never permanent growth-rate effects.

### 6.1 The OECD tax-and-growth ranking

- **Johansson, Heady, Arnold, Brys & Vartia (2008), OECD ECO WP 620.** The ranking runs from most to least harmful: corporate taxes, personal income taxes, consumption taxes, then **recurrent taxes on immovable property** (least harmful) [P abstract].
- **Arnold (2008), OECD ECO WP 643.** Pooled-mean-group panel of 21 OECD countries, 1971–2004. Recurrent immovable-property taxes have the largest positive coefficient relative to income taxes: 2.47 (SE 0.84), or 1.45 in another specification. The category "other property taxes", which includes transaction taxes, is insignificant (−0.34) [P]. The units of the revenue-share variable were not stated in the extract, so **I do not convert these coefficients into GDP percentages.**
- **Arnold, Brys, Heady, Johansson, Schwellnus & Vartia (2011), *Economic Journal* 121(550).** As quoted by O'Connor, Hynes, Haugh & Lenain (2016, *Econ. Soc. Review* 47(1)): "a revenue-neutral shift of 1 per cent of tax revenues from income taxes to consumption and property taxes would increase GDP per capita by between 0.25 per cent and 1 per cent in the long run" [S]. Note that this figure covers consumption and property **together**.

### 6.2 Robustness

- **Xing (2011 WP; 2012 *Economics Letters* 117(1)).** No robust ranking of tax types. The property-tax result is "the only robust result", but it is driven by "other property taxes", and "there is no evidence that recurrent taxes on immovable property are the most 'growth friendly'". The result fails in mean-group estimates [P WP].
- **Baiardi, Profeta, Puglisi & Scabrosetti (2019), *Int. Tax Public Finance* 26(2).** The OECD results replicate only with less conservative standard errors. They are not robust to more countries, longer periods, or short-run vs long-run splits [P abstract].
- **Acosta-Ormaechea, Sola & Yoo (2019), *German Economic Review* 20(4).** Across 70 countries, moving revenue from income taxes to consumption and property taxes is associated with higher long-run growth in high- and middle-income countries [P abstract]. This supports the OECD direction, still from cross-country regressions.
- **Goodhart, Hudson, Kumhof & Tideman (2021), WP.** A calibrated US model of a very large shift (land tax from 0.55 % to 5.55 %, cutting capital and labour income tax rates by 28 and 10 pp) yields output about 15 % above trend [P abstract]. The result is model-driven and the reform is of a different order of magnitude. **It should not be cited as an estimate for these scenarios.**

### 6.3 Applicability to the scenarios

- **S1 swaps one property-related tax for another.** The OECD estimates are about income taxes versus property taxes and say nothing direct about replacing a transaction tax plus a building-inclusive recurrent tax with a land-only recurrent tax. The efficiency gain in S1 comes from two channels:
  1. **Removing the DMTO wedge.** The welfare cost is about 1/8 of revenue in Toronto and 12–15 % of revenue at a 2 % rate in the Helsinki model, rising steeply with the rate.
  2. **Untaxing structures.** Likely small given France's low supply elasticity.
- **Illustrative S1 arithmetic.** *This is my back-of-envelope, not a literature estimate.* Applying the Toronto ratio (1/8) to about €16bn of DMTO gives about €2bn/yr of welfare, roughly 0.07 % of GDP (France's GDP is about €2,900bn). Welfare is not GDP, and the French 2014 evidence of no lasting volume effect points to a lower bound near zero. **Do not publish as a range.**
- **S2 and S3** do move revenue from (capital) income taxes to a recurrent property tax: about €3.7bn (S2) to €13.7bn (S3 with IFI), per `yields.py` after the Gemini review. That is roughly 0.3–1 % of total compulsory levies, which are about 43 % of GDP. A naive application of the Arnold et al. (2011) range would suggest a long-run GDP-per-capita level gain of a few tenths of a percent at most. Three reasons make it unreliable:
  - the estimate concerns income taxes in general, mostly on labour;
  - it bundles property with consumption taxes;
  - it does not survive the robustness checks above.
- **Lock-in and misallocation.** Lock-in costs from capital-gains taxes (Shan 2011) and transfer-tax misallocation (Määttänen & Terviö) are real mechanisms. No French GDP quantification exists.

**Credibility.** Weak. Recommended site treatment: **direction positive but small, not quantified.** Optionally cite the OECD range as context, with an explicit warning.

## 7. Effect 6: Distributional and behavioural side effects

### 7.1 Liquidity of land-rich, income-poor owners (all scenarios; most acute in S1)

**Mechanism.** An LVT moves the burden from buildings and transactions to land value. Owners whose land is valuable relative to their income face higher annual bills they may not be able to pay from income:

- retirees in high-value areas;
- farmers, depending on how the LVT treats agricultural land now under the TFPNB;
- heirs of property in pressured markets.

**Existing French relief on the TFPB** [P: LégiFiscal, citing BOI-BAREME-000006]:

- exemptions for recipients of certain minimum benefits (art. 1390 CGI);
- exemption for low-income owners aged 75 and over (art. 1391);
- a €100 automatic relief for low-income owners aged 65–75 (art. 1391 B);
- an income-based cap that relieves the part of the tax above 50 % of income for owners not liable to the IFI, under the art. 1417-II thresholds (art. 1391 B ter).

Whether these carry over to an LVT is a design choice.

**Deferral elsewhere.**

- The OECD (2022) recommends deferral options for low-income households and retirees [P brochure].
- Denmark lets owners freeze increases in land tax (since 2018) and property-value tax (from 2024). From 2024 the frozen amount is an **interest-bearing loan** (3.57 % for 2024) repaid at sale [S: The Local DK, Nov. 2023]. Take-up was not found.
- US deferral programmes have "very low take-up" (Munnell et al., CRR Brief 17-19, 2017) [P; no figures given].

**Direction.** Higher liquidity risk for a minority of owners, mitigable by design. **Credibility:** strong mechanism. Size depends on French microdata (land values against incomes), which the static model could compute if household incomes are linked.

### 7.2 Removal of capital-gains lock-in (S2)

See §2.3. Turnover of long-held non-main-residence assets rises. A one-off sales surge is likely right after removal, and a delay before it if the removal is announced in advance.

- US: +19–24 % sales rate for affected homes, mostly short run (Shan 2011).
- France: lock-in "peu étayée" (Cour des comptes 2025). The current holding-period allowance (cost about €4.8bn in 2022) and main-residence exemption (about €5.6bn in 2022) already create strong "wait" incentives for non-main residences [P Cour summary].

**Credibility.** Moderate abroad, weak in France.

### 7.3 IFI-related wealth relocation and emigration (S2 toggle)

**Evidence, France Stratégie, *Comité d'évaluation des réformes de la fiscalité du capital – rapport final* (October 2023)** [P]:

- **Departures and returns.** Under the ISF (2011–2016) there were about 950 departures and 370 returns per year. Under the IFI (2018–2021) there were about 260 departures and 380 returns per year.
- **The comparison is not like-for-like.** The IFI population is much smaller than the ISF population was: about 133,000–164,000 IFI payers.
- **Interpretation.** The numbers are "de petits effectifs, de l'ordre de quelques centaines". A causal effect cannot be rigorously established, and the budget impact is "vraisemblablement de second ordre".
- **Portfolios.** No shift away from real estate has been observed after 2018.
- **Yield.** IFI yield was €1.29bn in 2018 and €1.83bn in 2022. *The €2.2bn figure in the scenario brief was not verified here.*

**Wider literature.**

- Brülhart et al. (2022): a 1 pp wealth-tax cut raises reported wealth by at least 43 % after six years in two Swiss cantons. About 24 % of that comes from mobility and about 20 % from house-price capitalisation; the remainder points to evasion of self-reported financial wealth [P].
- This transfers poorly to the IFI, which bears on **real estate**: immobile, and valued with third-party information.
- The elasticity of taxable income literature (Saez, Slemrod & Giertz 2012, *JEL* 50(1)) is the general framework. *I could not verify its summary range in the full text, so no number is given.*

**Key point for the site.** An LVT cannot be escaped by moving abroad, because the land stays. Owners can, but their land remains taxed. Replacing the IFI with an LVT therefore trades a small, uncertain relocation incentive for a tax on an immobile base. For many former IFI payers the LVT bill may be higher, not lower.

**Credibility.** Weak, and the effect is small in either direction.

### 7.4 Other flags

- **Announcement and transition timing (all).** French 2014 evidence: +26 % transactions in the month before a DMTO rise (Bérard & Trannoy). By symmetry, an announced future DMTO abolition would likely **freeze transactions** until it takes effect. Capitalisation of the LVT happens **at announcement** (Høj et al.), not gradually.
- **Renovation incentives (S3).** Abolishing taxation of rents abolishes the déficit foncier deduction, a current incentive for landlord renovation works including energy renovation. Direction ↓ for that incentive. Not quantified.
- **Furnished and short-term lets (S3).** If furnished-let income (BIC) is included, check that S3 does not create a new tax advantage for short-term over long-term lets. The OECD (2022) recommends that short-term rental income not be taxed more favourably [P].
- **Local government behaviour.** The TF is a communal resource and the DMTO a départemental one. Replacing them changes local fiscal incentives (for example, zoning decisions that raise land values would raise LVT revenue). This is a political-economy effect. No French evidence was found.

## 8. What the site should say (proposed French wording)

*Format per effect: **Sens**, **Mécanisme** (two lines), **Ordre de grandeur**, **Fiabilité**. The tone is neutral and avoids evaluative adjectives.*

**1. Transactions et mobilité résidentielle**

- **Sens :** hausse attendue des ventes et des déménagements (S1, S2, S3) ; la suppression de l'imposition des plus-values (S2) peut y ajouter un pic temporaire de ventes.
- **Mécanisme :** les droits de mutation sont payés à chaque vente et freinent les déménagements ; une taxe annuelle sur le terrain ne dépend pas de la vente.
- **Ordre de grandeur :** à l'étranger, environ +6 % à +15 % de ventes par point de droits supprimé (Allemagne, Toronto). En France, la hausse de 2014 (+0,7 point) n'a eu qu'un effet temporaire. Pas d'extrapolation fiable à une suppression totale (environ 6 points).
- **Fiabilité :** sens établi ; ampleur incertaine pour la France.

**2. Construction et usage du sol**

- **Sens :** plutôt favorable à la construction et à la densification ; effet incertain sur les terrains constructibles selon leur mode d'évaluation.
- **Mécanisme :** le bâti n'est plus taxé, et détenir un terrain sous-utilisé coûte plus cher.
- **Ordre de grandeur :** non chiffrable pour la France ; à l'étranger, de nul à environ +12 % de mises en chantier selon les études. En France, les règles d'urbanisme limitent souvent davantage l'offre que la fiscalité.
- **Fiabilité :** faible.

**3. Loyers**

- **Sens :** pas d'effet direct attendu à court terme ; légère baisse possible à long terme si l'offre locative augmente.
- **Mécanisme :** un impôt sur le terrain ne peut pas être répercuté sur les locataires, car la quantité de terrain ne change pas ; la suppression de l'impôt sur les loyers (S3) se retrouve d'abord dans le prix des biens.
- **Ordre de grandeur :** non chiffrable. *(Les effets sur les aides au logement font l'objet d'une note distincte.)*
- **Fiabilité :** absence de répercussion de l'impôt foncier : solide ; effet de la suppression des impôts sur les bailleurs : faible.

**4. Prix des terrains et des logements**

- **Sens :** à recettes égales, la valeur de l'ensemble des biens change peu ; elle se redistribue. Les biens où le terrain pèse le plus perdent, ceux où le bâti domine gagnent. Dans les scénarios 2 et 3, les biens loués ou détenus pour être revendus gagnent et les résidences principales perdent, car elles ne payaient ni impôt sur les plus-values ni impôt sur les loyers.
- **Mécanisme :** les acheteurs intègrent dans le prix les impôts futurs ; ceux qui possèdent au moment de l'annonce supportent l'essentiel de l'effet, en perte comme en gain.
- **Ordre de grandeur :** les études trouvent qu'entre 60 % et 100 % de la valeur actualisée d'un impôt foncier se retrouve dans les prix (100 % au Danemark pour l'impôt sur le terrain).
- **Fiabilité :** solide pour l'impôt sur le terrain ; moyenne pour les données françaises.

**5. Niveau du PIB à long terme**

- **Sens :** effet de niveau positif possible, probablement faible.
- **Mécanisme :** les impôts sur les transactions et sur le capital immobilier découragent les échanges et l'investissement ; un impôt sur le terrain modifie peu les comportements.
- **Ordre de grandeur :** non chiffrable de façon fiable ; les estimations internationales (OCDE) reposent sur des comparaisons entre pays dont la robustesse est contestée.
- **Fiabilité :** faible.

**6a. Propriétaires disposant de faibles revenus**

- **Sens :** risque de difficultés de paiement pour certains propriétaires dont le terrain a une forte valeur au regard de leurs revenus.
- **Mécanisme :** l'impôt dépend de la valeur du terrain, pas du revenu.
- **Ordre de grandeur :** dépend des mécanismes d'atténuation (plafonnement en fonction du revenu, report du paiement jusqu'à la vente, comme au Danemark).
- **Fiabilité :** mécanisme établi ; ampleur non mesurée.

**6b. Plus-values (S2)**

- **Sens :** davantage de ventes de biens détenus depuis longtemps.
- **Mécanisme :** sans imposition, il n'y a plus d'intérêt à attendre l'exonération pour durée de détention.
- **Ordre de grandeur :** États-Unis : +19 % à +24 % de ventes dans le groupe concerné ; en France, la Cour des comptes juge cet effet « peu étayé ».
- **Fiabilité :** faible pour la France.

**6c. IFI et expatriation (option S2)**

- **Sens :** légère baisse de l'incitation à l'expatriation si l'IFI est supprimé.
- **Mécanisme :** le terrain ne peut pas être déplacé ; l'impôt sur le terrain reste dû même si le propriétaire part.
- **Ordre de grandeur :** quelques centaines de départs par an ; effet budgétaire « de second ordre » (France Stratégie, 2023).
- **Fiabilité :** faible.

**6d. Calendrier**

- **Sens :** une suppression annoncée à l'avance peut geler les ventes jusqu'à son entrée en vigueur ; la baisse du prix des terrains intervient dès l'annonce.
- **Ordre de grandeur :** en 2014, +26 % de ventes le mois précédant une hausse des droits.
- **Fiabilité :** solide sur le sens.

## 9. Gaps and transfers of international evidence

- **No French estimate exists** for:
  - the long-run effect of large DMTO changes;
  - the effect of land taxation on construction;
  - the pass-through of income taxes on rents to tenants;
  - the GDP effect of any of these swaps.
- **The 2025 départemental DMTO rise** (+0.5 pp in most départements, with optional first-time-buyer relief) is a usable natural experiment. No evaluation was found as of October 2026.
- **Valuation.** Results depend heavily on how the LVT values buildable and agricultural land. The current TFPNB rests on dated cadastral values, so moving to market land values changes incentives far more than the headline yield suggests.
- **Data the Cour des comptes says is missing:** DGFiP access to notarial databases and a flag for main-residence exemptions. These would be needed to estimate S2 behaviour in France.

## Review note (house view, 26 October 2026)

Reviewed by the lead session against spec 0005 and `yields.py`. Changes from the agent's draft:

1. **Capitalisation framing (§1.1 rows 4a–4b, §1.2 point 2, §5.4, §8 item 4).** The draft said land prices fall in S1. At equal yield that is incomplete: the DMTO and the building share of the TFPB disappear at the same time, and their removal capitalises too. The house view is that total property values change little on average and are redistributed by land share (S1) and from owner-occupiers to landlords and investors (S2, S3). Whether the land *component* falls depends on the horizon; the site's wording is pending the discussion on PR #40.
2. **Yields** in §4.3 and §6.3 now quote `yields.py`.
3. **IFI.** The draft's €1.83bn for 2022 (France Stratégie) differs from the 2,1 Md€ in `yields.py` (CPO). Passed to the yield verification pass; `yields.py` uses only the latest year.

4. **Rents, reconciled with the APL note.** This note says the rent effect of S3 is "not quantifiable"; `apl-rent-incidence.md` proposes a pass-through parameter (long run 0,5, range 0,25–0,75; years 1–3: 0,1). Both stand: no French study measures it, so the site shows it as a parameter with a wide range, labelled as an inference from incidence theory and foreign evidence, never as a French estimate. The new land tax itself has a pass-through of zero.
