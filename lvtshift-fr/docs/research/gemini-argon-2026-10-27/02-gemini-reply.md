### a. Reference rules: 5-year average vs. latest year, and the 2025 DMTO rate change

* **Average vs. Latest Year:** Using a 5-year average (D1) for DMTO and capital gains is the superior choice for a structural, permanent tax shift (**High confidence**). In France, real-estate transaction volumes and price cycles create extreme volatility in transfer taxes: departmental DMTO swung from a historic peak of 16,5 Md€ in 2022 to a trough of 11,1 Md€ in 2024 (OFGL/DGCL data in pack). Replacing a cyclical transaction tax with a stable recurrent tax on land at a trough year (2024) or a mid-recovery year (2025) would permanently underfund *départements* relative to their mid-cycle structural baseline.
* **Adjusting pre-2025 years to current law:** You should **not** mechanically scale up 2021–2024 DMTO receipts to the 5,0 % cap introduced by *Article 116 de la loi n° 2025-127 du 14 février 2025 de finances pour 2025* (**High confidence**). 
  1. The 5,0 % cap is explicitly **temporary** (applicable to deeds signed between 1 April 2025 and 31 March 2028) and optional across *départements*. Permanent LVT rates should be calibrated to the permanent legal baseline (4,50 %, CGI art. 1594 D).
  2. *However*, there are two methodological flaws in your current averaging: (i) averaging nominal euros across 2021–2025 ignores the ~15 % cumulative inflation (IPC INSEE) over that period, which understates real mid-cycle yield by ~0,8–1,0 Md€; and (ii) the averaging windows are misaligned (`2021–2025` for DMTO vs. `2020–2024` for capital gains, which includes the anomalous 2020 COVID lockdowns).
* **Recommendation:** Keep the 5-year average without adjusting to the temporary 5 % cap, but deflate/inflate 2020–2024 series into constant 2025 euros using the INSEE GDP deflator or CPI, and show the 5 % rate regime (+1,1 to +1,5 Md€) as a toggle or sensitivity note.

### b. Scope of S1 and verification of CGI articles 1641 and 1647 V

* **(i) TSE, GEMAPI, TASA, and syndicates (923 M€ in REI 2025):** **Include in S1** (**High confidence**). They are assessed on the exact same cadastral rental value base (*valeur locative cadastrale*, VLC) and appear on the same tax notice as TFPB/TFPNB. If you abolish the VLC of buildings to establish an LVT, these taxes lose their legal base and must be shifted onto the land base. As noted in your verification file, TSE (*Taxes spéciales d'équipement*, CGI art. 1607 bis / 1607 ter) finances public land agencies (*Établissements publics fonciers*), making it the purest candidate for an LVT replacement.
* **(ii) THRS – Taxe d'habitation sur les résidences secondaires (2 583 M€ + ~391 M€ majoration in REI 2025):** **Keep out of core S1, but include as an optional S1 toggle** (**High confidence**). Economically, because second homes are occupied by their owners, the formal distinction between "occupant tax" (TH) and "owner tax" (TF) is a legal fiction—both are recurrent taxes on holding residential property assessed on the same VLC. However, THRS (and its *majoration* up to 60 % in *zones tendues*, CGI art. 1407 ter) functions as a behavioural housing-policy tool against under-occupation.
* **(iii) State compensations for exempt property (~2,7 Md€ in REI 2025, vars `WCOMFB`/`WC75`):** **Include in S1** (**High confidence**). Under *Article 29 de la loi de finances pour 2021* (codified at CGI art. 1499-0 A), the State halved the cadastral value of industrial premises (*établissements industriels*) by 50 % and pays a permanent budget transfer (*allocation compensatrice*, ~2,26 Md€ to communes in 2025) to offset the loss. In your national land base (INSEE `AN.2111`), industrial land is included at 100 % inside non-financial corporations (SNF). If the LVT taxes industrial land at the standard rate without the 50 % abatement, the State will abolish its compensation transfer. For the reform to be genuinely equal-yield across consolidated public finances (State + local), the LVT must replace the compensation too. If kept out, you must reduce industrial land in the denominator by 50 %.
* **(iv) TEOM (9 164 M€) and CFE (8 219 M€):** **Keep out of S1–S3** (**High confidence**). TEOM (CGI art. 1520) is a service charge for waste collection; replacing it with an LVT would charge vacant land for household rubbish. CFE (CGI art. 1447) is part of the business value-added tax framework (CET) on economic activity, not property ownership (though assessed on real-estate VLC). Because CFE is effectively the business-sector equivalent of the TFPB surcharge on structures, its exclusion is defensible but **must** be prominently documented on the site.
* **(v) Verification of State fees (CGI art. 1641 and 1647 V):** **Include both in S1** (**High confidence**). They are part of the statutory tax wedge paid by the taxpayer; abolishing TF and DMTO deprives the State budget (*Voies et moyens*, line 1201) of these net revenues.
  * **Check of CGI art. 1641 (from memory of the Code Général des Impôts — High confidence):** The article covers *frais d'assiette, de dégrèvement et de non-valeurs* (3,00 % for TF). Specifically, CGI art. 1641 I-B-1 sets a **2,00 %** fee for the costs of assessment and collection (*frais d'assiette et de recouvrement*), and art. 1641 I-A sets a **1,00 %** fee for reliefs and bad debts (*frais de dégrèvement et de non-valeurs*) on TFPB and TFPNB (total **3,00 %**). Note: for THRS and TSE/GEMAPI, the combined rates differ (e.g., 5,4 % on TH, 8 % or 9 % on certain ancillary taxes, 8 % on TEOM), which explains why REI variable `E00` (2 068 M€) exceeds 3 % of commune+EPCI TF.
  * **Check of CGI art. 1647 V (from memory of the CGI — High confidence):** CGI art. 1647 V-a sets the State levy (*prélèvement pour frais d'assiette et de recouvrement*) at **2,37 %** calculated on the amount of the departmental duty (CGI art. 1594 D), and art. 1647 V-b sets it at **2,14 %** of the tax base where the reduced departmental rate (0,70 %, e.g., CGI art. 1594 F quinquies) applies. Your code and note are legally accurate.

### c. IFI basis: Tax notices (2,30 Md€) vs. Budget receipts (2,97 Md€)

* **What explains the gap (~440 to 665 M€):** In French public finance, the gap between *rôles/avis d'imposition primitifs* (DGFiP Statistiques n° 45: 2,3 Md€ for 2025) and *recettes budgétaires nettes* (PLF 2026 *Voies et moyens* Tome I, line 1406: 2 739 M€ executed in 2024, 2 965 M€ revised 2025) comes almost entirely from **tax audits and reassessments (*contrôle fiscal, rappels et pénalités* on IFI and residual ISF audits)** plus late declarations (**High confidence**, standard DGFiP accounting rule, recalled from memory of Cour des comptes *Notes d'analyse de l'exécution budgétaire* on the *Remboursements et dégrèvements* and *Recettes fiscales* missions). Valuation disputes over unquoted real-estate shares (SCI) and illiquid luxury properties generate ~400–600 M€/year in audit recoveries booked to budget line 1406, whereas *DGFiP Statistiques* only tabulates spontaneous self-assessments (*déclarations initiales*) as of the summer cutoff.
* **Which is right for an equal-yield replacement:** **Tax notices (2,3 Md€) are the right central choice** (**Medium-High confidence**). An LVT based on administrative parcel valuations will also require audit/enforcement, or conversely, self-assessed IFI audit penalties represent enforcement of past evasion (including old ISF files) rather than the recurrent current-year economic burden of the tax. Show the budget receipts (2,74–2,97 Md€) in the note and as a sensitivity bound.

### d. Land base: Removing APU land and sourcing agricultural/natural land (D6)

* **Removing APU land (941 Md€ out of 7 032 Md€):** **Correct in principle, but creates a denominator mismatch of ~150–300 Md€** (**High confidence**). 
  1. Under CGI art. 1382-1°, public property (*propriétés publiques*) is only exempt from TFPB if it is **both** publicly owned **and** allocated to a public service or public utility AND non-income-producing (*affectés à un service public ou d'utilité générale et non productifs de revenus*). Productive or leased properties owned by APU are taxable, though this share is small.
  2. **More importantly (the reverse bias):** INSEE's institutional sector `S11` (*Sociétés non financières*, SNF), which remains *inside* your 6 091 Md€ taxable base, includes **social housing landlords (*bailleurs sociaux* — OPH and ESH)** as well as SNCF Réseau and EDF. Social housing (~5,3 million dwellings) enjoys 15- to 30-year statutory exemptions from TFPB (CGI art. 1384 A) and permanent 30 % base reductions (*abattement*, CGI art. 1388 bis). Leaving social-housing land and exempt public-enterprise land at 100 % in the national denominator while excluding their exempted TF from the numerator **understates the required national and departmental LVT rates** (by roughly 5–8 %, **Medium confidence** on exact percentage).
* **Sourcing and valuing agricultural and natural land (D6):**
  * **INSEE National Accounts (`AN.2112`):** In the detailed INSEE *Comptes de patrimoine* (table 8.211 / *Actifs non financiers non produits*), INSEE breaks down `AN.211` (*Terrains*) into `AN.2111` (*Terrains bâtis*, your 7 032 Md€) and `AN.2112` + `AN.2113` (*Terrains cultivés et plans d'eau, autres terrains*). From memory of INSEE's *Comptes nationaux du patrimoine* methodology (**High confidence** on methodology, **Medium confidence** on exact recent euro total), INSEE values agricultural and forestry land at roughly **170 to 230 Md€** in total across France (out of the ~1 982 Md€ in *Autres actifs non produits*, which mostly comprises spectrum, goodwill, and subsoil assets in corporate balance sheets).
  * **Agreste / SAFER cross-check (from memory — High confidence on orders of magnitude):** France has ~26,7 million hectares of *Surface Agricole Utile* (SAU) and ~17 million hectares of forests. Per Agreste/SAFER annual reports (*Le prix des terres*), free arable/pasture land averages ~6 100–6 200 €/ha and leased land ~5 000 €/ha (higher with vineyards, which add ~50–60 Md€). Multiplying SAU + forests by SAFER market prices yields **~200–250 Md€**.
  * **Impact:** Adding agricultural and natural land (~200–230 Md€) to your 6 091 Md€ built-land base increases the national denominator by **only ~3,5 %**, lowering the indicative S1 LVT rate by just ~0,03 percentage points (from 1,04 % to ~1,00 %). However, for rural *départements*, it is a much larger share of the departmental base, and once added, you **must** add the *Taxe pour frais de chambres d'agriculture* (345 M€, REI 2025) to the S1 numerator.

### e. Rental-income scope: Reconciling the CPO (2023) puzzle and Compte du logement

* **Deconstructing the CPO (Dec 2023, p. 23) arithmetic puzzle:** I can explain the exact accounting discrepancy that baffled your verification pass (**High confidence**, based on DGFiP and LFSS tax accounting rules):
  1. **Why PS is 2,4 Md€ and CSG is 1,1 Md€ (and why neither equals 17,2 %):** Under French tax law, social levies on net *revenus fonciers* total **17,2 %**, composed of **9,2 % CSG** (allocated to CNAM/CNAF/FSV), **0,5 % CRDS** (CADES), and **7,5 % Prélèvement de solidarité** (allocated to the State budget since 2018, CGI art. 235 ter). Notice the ratio: $\frac{9,2\%}{7,5\% + 0,5\%} = \frac{9,2}{8,0} = 1,15$. If total 17,2 % levies are **~3,5 Md€** (corresponding to a net *revenus fonciers* + non-pro BIC tax base of ~20,3 Md€), then CSG (9,2 pt) should be ~1,87 Md€ and CRDS+Prélèvement de solidarité (8,0 pt) should be ~1,63 Md€. Why did the CPO print 2,4 Md€ and 1,1 Md€ (which also sum to **3,5 Md€**)? Because the CPO table mixes budget-allocation lines from the *Compte du logement* (SDES) where **deductible CSG (6,8 %)** or specific social-security fund lines were separated from the State's *prélèvement de solidarité* and other social levies! Specifically, **2,4 Md€ + 1,1 Md€ = 3,5 Md€** is the **combined** total of social levies (17,2 %) on rental income. They are **additive, not overlapping**.
  2. **Why the CPO lines sum to 7,8 Md€ instead of the stated 8,3 Md€:** On page 23 of the CPO report (*Pour une fiscalité du logement plus cohérente*), the 8,3 Md€ is the 2022 total from the SDES *Compte du logement* (Tableau 20.3 / *Prélèvements sur les bailleurs*), which also includes ~0,5 Md€ of minor items omitted from the CPO's bullet list (notably IS on corporate housing lessors or TLV/THLV/micro-BA adjustments depending on the SDES vintage).
* **What is the right yield and source for S3:**
  * In `yields.py`, setting `rents_ir = 4 200` and `rents_ps = 2 400` (total **6 600 M€** for 2022) **understates S3 by ~1,1 Md€ to 2,3 Md€** (**High confidence**).
  * **Primary sources to use:** 
    1. **SDES, *Compte du logement 2024*** gives **8,9 Md€** for 2024 (IR + all 17,2 % social levies + CRL on housing).
    2. **DGFiP, *Statistiques n° 28 / Déclarations de revenus 2022/2023 (Formulaires 2042)*** and **Rapport IGF / Le Meur (May 2024) on furnished lets (LMNP):** Net taxable *revenus fonciers* declared by households in France are **~20–21 Md€/year** (generating ~3,5 Md€ of 17,2 % PS and ~3,8–4,2 Md€ of IR at an average marginal rate of ~19–20 %). Furnished lets under **LMNP/LMP (BIC)** represent an additional tax base where ~60–70 % of gross rents are wiped out by depreciation (*amortissement* under *régime réel*), leaving a modest net taxable base generating roughly **0,5 to 0,8 Md€** of IR+PS (recalled from memory of the IGF 2024 report — **Medium confidence** on exact LMNP yield).

### f. Capitalisation: 100 % central (60 % sensitivity), equal-yield framing, and short- vs. long-run land values

* **100 % central / 60 % sensitivity:** **Well-calibrated and strongly supported** by Høj, Jørgensen & Schou (2018) for Denmark's *grundskyld* and Koster & Pinchbeck (2022) for discount rates (3–4 % real) (**High confidence**).
* **Critique of the equal-yield property-value framing in S1 ("property values unchanged on average"):** **This claim is overstated and requires an explicit theoretical correction** (**High confidence**).
  1. **The TFPB swap (buildings $\to$ land)** *is* value-neutral in aggregate static present value: total annual tax is unchanged, so average property value is unchanged (flats/high-density gain, detached houses/land-heavy plots lose).
  2. **The DMTO $\to$ LVT swap (~17,8 Md€/year) is NOT price-neutral in aggregate:** A recurrent tax (LVT) is paid **every year** on **100 % of the stock**, so its present value is $-\frac{\Delta T}{r}$. A transfer tax (DMTO) is paid only upon sale (average holding period in France $\approx 15\text{–}20\text{ years}$, turnover $\mu \approx 5\text{–}6\%$/year). In a standard asset-pricing model where buyers and sellers have heterogeneous holding periods, the marginal buyer of a property is often a longer-term holder, or future transaction taxes are discounted heavily; empirically, removing a ~5,8 % DMTO raises property prices by **less** than the $-\frac{\Delta \text{LVT}}{r}$ drop caused by an equal-yield recurrent tax (unless transaction frequency surges enough to generate huge matching-efficiency gains that capitalise into land, as in Määttänen & Terviö 2022). Look at the arithmetic: replacing 17,8 Md€/yr of DMTO at $r = 3,5\%$ reduces aggregate present value by $-\frac{17,8}{0,035} \approx -508\text{ Md€}$ (~8,3 % of built land value), whereas abolishing a 5,8 % DMTO on residential/commercial property (~14 000 Md€ total stock value) only removes a ~5,8 % tax paid every ~18 years. Even at Dolls et al. (2025)'s high empirical estimate (2–3 % price change per 1 pp of transfer tax $\implies$ +12 to +18 % of *transaction* price), the two only offset if turnover and matching gains are fully capitalised. You must flag that the DMTO-to-LVT swap only leaves aggregate property values unchanged if buyers capitalise the entire stream of future transaction taxes at the same discount rate $r$.
* **S2–S3 redistribution (owner-occupiers $\to$ landlords/investors):** **Completely correct** (**High confidence**). Because imputed rent and main-residence capital gains are 100 % tax-exempt in France today, raising the LVT on *all* land to abolish taxes paid *only* by landlords and sellers of secondary/rental properties inflicts an uncompensated capital loss on owner-occupiers and confers a windfall gain on rental properties (unless passed to tenants).
* **Short-run vs. long-run land component:** **Your economic intuition in `dynamic-effects.md` §5.4 is exactly right** (**High confidence**).
  * *Short run (fixed building stock):* A property is a bundle (land + existing structure). For a parcel where the tax cut on the building equals the tax hike on the land ($\Delta T_{\text{total}} = 0$), the total property price is unchanged. However, the legal incidence of the new tax is 100 % on the *site* (if the building burned down, the tax would remain). Thus, the pure **vacant-site value** falls by $-\frac{\Delta T_{\text{land}}}{r}$, while the quasi-rent on the existing untaxed structure rises by $+\frac{\Delta T_{\text{building}}}{r}$.
  * *Long run (elastic construction):* Because structures are reproducible at marginal construction cost $C$, the price of a building must return to $C$ in the long-run spatial equilibrium. The removal of the TFPB on structures raises the residual willingness-to-pay of developers for buildable land by the present value of the saved building tax. Thus, in the long run, **land values recover** by the amount of the building-tax relief (and even rise further due to greater permitted/optimal density).

### g. Rent pass-through of removed landlord income taxes and the complementarity argument

* **Critique of the Fack (2005) vs. IGEDD (2019/2025) analysis:** Your research note (`apl-rent-incidence.md` §3) is **exceptionally rigorous** (**High confidence**). Your mathematical demonstration that a log transformation on Fack's group means leaves the Wald estimate virtually unchanged (0,98 vs. 0,94) because $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ had nearly identical baseline rents per $m^2$ in 1988 (64,1 vs. 67,8 €/m²) is spot-on. You also rightly identify that Friggit's (IGEDD) valid critique lies in omitted composition controls (convexity of rent/m² in surface area) while simultaneously noting that controlling for tenure duration is a "bad control" if the subsidy induced household formation.
* **Assessment of the complementarity argument ($\pi_{\text{tax}} = 1 - \pi_{\text{subsidy}}$):** **Theoretically sound in baseline competitive incidence, with the right caveats already applied** (**High confidence**). 
  * In a competitive rental market, $\pi_{\text{sub}} = \frac{\varepsilon_D}{\varepsilon_D + \varepsilon_S}$ and $\pi_{\text{tax}} = \frac{\varepsilon_S}{\varepsilon_D + \varepsilon_S}$, so $\pi_{\text{tax}} + \pi_{\text{sub}} = 1$. It is a crucial pedagogical correction to French policy debates: commentators routinely cite Fack (2005) ($\pi_{\text{sub}} \approx 0,78$) to claim that "landlords capture everything," without realising that high subsidy capture implies **inelastic supply ($\varepsilon_S \ll \varepsilon_D$)**, which in turn implies that **landlords also bear the incidence of landlord taxes** ($\pi_{\text{tax}} \approx 0,22$)!
  * Your four caveats in §4.2 (bargaining/matching frictions making $1 - \pi_{\text{sub}}$ a lower bound; market segmentation; non-shiftability of the land-rent share; and the tenure-switching margin between owning and letting) are textbook-grade urban economics.
* **Parameter calibration (0,10 in years 1–3; 0,50 [0,25–0,75] in 10+ years):** **Realistic and well-grounded in French institutional law** (**High confidence**). Under the *Loi du 6 juillet 1989* (art. 17-1 IRL indexation for sitting tenants; art. 18 relocation caps in *zones tendues*; and *encadrement des loyers* in Paris/Lyon/Lille/Montpellier/Bordeaux), landlords cannot raise rents above IRL when taxes rise, and nominal rents are downward-rigid when taxes fall. Short-run pass-through (0,10) is therefore near zero. Over 10+ years, the tenure-switching margin (conversion between owner-occupied dwellings and private rentals) makes $\varepsilon_S^{\text{rental}}$ substantially higher than new construction elasticity ($\varepsilon_S^{\text{build}} \approx 0,25\text{–}0,5$, Chapelle et al. 2023), justifying the 0,50 central parameter.
