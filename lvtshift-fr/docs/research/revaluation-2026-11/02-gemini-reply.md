# Gemini's reply, 11 November 2026

Kept verbatim. What we took and rejected is in README.md: its elasticity of 0.55 and its claim that French studies "consistently show" it are unsourced, and contradicted by the evidence in 03-literature.md.

**a) Market value as a proxy for the RVLLH**
Using raw parcel market value is wrong and will severely overstate the redistributive effect of the revision. The RVLLH (Art. 74 of the 2014 Finance Law, modified by the 2020 Finance Law) mirrors the 2017 RVLLP: it divides communes into homogeneous rental sectors, divides housing into 5 size categories, and applies a reference rent per weighted square meter (*tarif au m² pondéré*), adjusted by a parcel-specific *coefficient de localisation* bounded between 0.7 and 1.3. Because rental yields compress sharply as property prices rise—and because rents per $m^2$ fall steeply with dwelling size while prices per $m^2$ do not—market value is vastly more dispersed than rental value. Do not use the *Carte des loyers* (it is commune-level, thus useless for within-commune variance). Instead, apply a concave elasticity to your per-$m^2$ market value: $\text{Weight} = \text{Surface} \times (\text{Market Value} / \text{Surface})^b$. French micro-data (OLAP, Clameur, and the 2017 IPP/IGF studies on the 5-department RVLLH experiment) consistently show a rent-to-price elasticity across neighborhoods and sizes of $b \approx 0.5$ to $0.6$. Use $b = 0.55$; using $b = 1.0$ turns a rental tax into a capital wealth tax and invalidates the counterfactual.

**b) Honesty of the baseline and labelling**
Calling your first step the "effect of the RVLLH" is scientifically dishonest and will get your paper rejected by anyone who knows French *fiscalité locale*. The 1970 *valeur locative cadastrale* (VLC) is not a flat per-$m^2$ tax; it heavily weights structural quality (8 categories from luxury to dilapidated), comfort elements (running water, central heating, bathrooms added as physical $m^2$ equivalents), and location. Its famous pathology is that 1970 *haussmannien* flats lacked modern comfort and were rated low (categories 4–6), while 1970s peripheral *grands ensembles* had modern plumbing and elevators and were rated high (categories 2–3), locking in a regressive bias as inner cities gentrified. A flat floor-area baseline erases this regressive starting point—in fact, a pure $m^2$ tax is often *less* regressive than the real 1970 VLC in gentrified cities. You must label the three states: **1. Flat-area baseline (synthetic status quo)**, **2. Market-value-indexed housing tax**, and **3. Split-rate tax (TFVN)**. In French, call the first step *"Effet de la prise en compte des valeurs de marché (proxy de la révision)"*, never *"Effet de la RVLLH"*, and explicitly caveating that lacking the *Fichiers fonciers* (MAJIC) prevents you from capturing the 1970 comfort-category distortions.

**c) Inter-group neutralisation (*Locaux professionnels*, *industriels*, and *habitation*)**
Your design to keep the three tax groups' aggregate shares fixed is 100% correct and legally mandatory. When the RVLLP was implemented in 2017, the legislature introduced the *coefficient de neutralisation* (CGI Art. 1518 A *quinquies*) specifically to prevent the updated professional base from crushing commercial taxpayers while waiting for the housing revision. Under the current statutory roadmap for the RVLLH (now pushed to 2028 via the 2023 Finance Law), the neutralisation coefficients will be abolished only if a new mechanism (*prise en compte des équilibres entre sous-groupes*) does not replace them—and in practice, the DGFiP and Parliament always neutralize inter-group transfers via coefficient adjustments or *planiamiento/lissage* to prevent massive tax hikes on homeowners. Letting housing absorb the professional and industrial tax shares (especially after the 2021 50% cut to the industrial *méthode comptable* which the State compensates) would model a political fantasy and drown your spatial land-tax signal in a massive inter-sectoral shift.

**d) Order of decomposition and interaction terms**
Because your reform bundles three distinct shocks—revaluing the base, shifting the base to land (4:1), and abolishing social housing exemptions—the decomposition is path-dependent. However, you should only publish **one** sequential path in the main text: **Baseline $\rightarrow$ Revaluation $\rightarrow$ Exemption removal $\rightarrow$ Land shift (4:1)**. This is the only politically and economically logical order: a land tax requires observing market values first, so "shifting to land" on a 1970/floor-area base is meaningless. Do not clutter the paper with a Shapley value decomposition or interaction terms; the only nonlinear interaction in your setup is between the repeal of the social housing exemptions and the land-value shift, which you eliminate cleanly by making the repeal of CGI 1384 A/C and QPV abatements its own intermediate step (Step 2b) or by explicitly keeping social housing exemptions constant in the main decomposition and showing their removal as a separate sensitivity column.

**e) Hidden flaws that will break the decomposition**
*   **Parcel-level aggregation of co-ownerships (*copropriétés*) and land shares:** A dense parcel with 50 flats has the same *total* parcel market value as 10 luxury villas on the same land area, so your parcel-level weight treats them identically in the revaluation step. Worse, in your TFVN step, a *copropriété* has a high building-to-land ratio (low land share), whereas a single-family home (*pavillon*) has a high land share. If you map parcel-level changes to *household income quintiles* (presumably via Filosofi gridded data), you will misattribute the tax shock in mixed neighborhoods unless you disaggregate parcels into *lots* (dwellings).
*   **Residual improvement values (`Market - Land`):** Subtracting comparable site values from hedonic market values routinely produces negative or near-zero improvement values in low-demand communes or on large lots with old buildings (because of demolition costs and option value). If you do not floor improvement value at a realistic depreciated replacement cost (or cap the land share at, say, 80–90% for built parcels), your 4:1 tax will create absurd spikes in peri-urban areas that look like a "land tax effect" but are actually econometric measurement error.
*   **Mixed-use parcels:** Scaling parcel market value by `housing_floor / total_floor` assumes residential and ground-floor commercial $m^2$ have the same capital value. In urban centers, ground-floor retail is worth 2x–4x upper-floor housing per $m^2$. Your formula over-allocates value to housing in mixed-use central buildings, artificially inflating the "revaluation effect" in dense city centers.
*   **Social housing (*HLM*) and QPV in the TFVN:** Repealing the 15–30 year exemptions (1384 A) and the 30% QPV abatement inside the "land-tax effect" is a fatal mistake for your income-quintile analysis. Social housing is heavily concentrated in the bottom two income quintiles. If you repeal their exemptions in the final step, your results will show that the "land-tax effect" raises taxes on poor neighborhoods, when in fact that increase comes entirely from repealing social housing subsidies!

**Overall Recommendation**
Do not publish the two-step decomposition as currently written; it conflates the repeal of social housing exemptions with the shift to land taxation, and it uses raw capital values that exaggerate rental revaluation. Instead, implement a **three-step sequential decomposition**: 
1. **Revaluation effect:** Move from your floor-area baseline to a rent-proxied base using $\text{Floor Area} \times (\text{Price per } m^2)^{0.55}$ within the housing group (keeping group shares and HLM/QPV exemptions fixed), clearly labelled as a *transition to rent-indexed market values*, not the exact RVLLH. 
2. **Land-shift effect (pure):** Move from the rent-proxied base to the 4:1 split-rate TFVN *while keeping the HLM exemptions and QPV abatements active* (and merging the non-agricultural TFPNB into the main solve). 
3. **Base-broadening effect:** Repeal the HLM and QPV exemptions on the TFVN base. 
This three-step structure isolates the exact theoretical mechanisms you want to test and makes your income-quintile results bulletproof against critiques from French tax specialists.
